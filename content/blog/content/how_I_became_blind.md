## rise in the dark

Wow, you made it this far. This is one of the longer ones, so buckle up. It is going to be a bumpy ride.

## First Symptoms

Let me start right at the beginning. I was around 15 when I started getting frequent migraines. They were so bad that I would throw up. Pain medication barely touched the pain. Sometimes my vision would blur in class, and focusing felt like trying to read through fog. I complained about it at the children’s home where I grew up. You are going to hear that word a lot, because the children’s home played a huge role in my life.

Nothing really came of those complaints. I went for eye tests and was prescribed glasses. I wore them for about a year, and then one day they disappeared. I was moving to a new room along the corridor, and they just vanished. To this day, I have never found them.

When I had to repeat my eye exam a few years later, the optometrist told me my eyes were perfectly fine. He even asked why I had been given glasses in the first place. According to him, my vision was in mint condition. So that was that. My eyes were “fine”.

I carried on with life, keeping the headaches in check as best I could. But there was a twist. The caretakers at the children’s home were very strict and often refused to give me painkillers. They thought I was becoming addicted. That was never the case. When they refused, I would get sick, throw up, feel dizzy, and become extremely sensitive to light.

Eventually, they took me to the town’s GP. He suspected muscle pain or spasms, because the pain was mostly in my neck and upper back. He gave me medication for that. It did not help. We begged him to send me for scans. He did, but only X-rays. I still do not understand why. I spent an entire day at the hospital and walked away with no answers. The doctor doing the X-rays told me I should have been sent to neurology instead.

That was the end of doctor visits for a long time. I just carried on.

After school, I worked on a farm, as mentioned in a previous post called, -How it all started_. The headaches never really stopped, and they affected my performance. It gave the farmer a bad impression of my abilities. From there, you already know the rest, so I will not repeat it.

Covid came and went, and I was still coping. After Covid, while working at a pizza shop, I started getting double vision. It did not happen often, so I brushed it off. The headaches were still there, like background noise you learn to live with.

Then I changed jobs, and this is where things turned serious. I no longer had the strength I used to. Dizziness became more frequent, but I could still manage daily tasks like driving. When I started working on towers, I was fine for the first two months. Then everything collapsed. I would vomit for no reason, lose my balance, and fall. This was a high-risk job, and my boss told me straight that I needed to fix my health.

By then, I was visiting the doctor often. I had a medical scheme with unlimited GP visits, so I used it. The doctor kept telling me he was waiting for the medical aid to approve the next steps. He said he could not refer me to a state hospital until they opened it up.

When things got really bad and I lost my job, I phoned the medical aid myself. I wanted answers. That is when I found out they did not even cover MRI scans, which is exactly what I needed. I could not afford a private MRI, and at a state hospital you cannot get one without a referral.

I was completely stuck.

I just sat at home, watching my world slowly shrink.

One day, a retired staff member from the children’s home tried to call me. I tried to answer, but my vision was already very bad. I accidentally declined the call. When I phoned her back, I explained that I pressed the wrong button because I could not see properly. She asked why. I told her I was sick and that my vision was so bad I could barely see my phone screen anymore.

She panicked and came to see me immediately. She took me to the same doctor I had been seeing and insisted on speaking to him herself. That led nowhere. Then we tried another option. The children’s home uses a GP in a nearby mall who consults their kids for free.

Within five minutes of looking into my eyes and asking a few questions, he said this was serious. No hesitation. No doubt. He told us to go to emergency immediately.

This time, we went to the state hospital with a proper referral. They took blood tests to check my kidneys so they could do a CT scan or MRI. A week later, I had the CT scan and was admitted to hospital. Because it was still Covid time, I was quarantined for three days.

After those three days, the doctor came with the news. He explained that there was an abnormality on my brain. They did not know exactly what it was, but it had to be removed as soon as possible.

I asked what would happen if I said no. He looked at me and told me I had less than three months to live. This was an emergency.

He said I could think about it and that he would come back later. That hour felt endless. I debated with myself. What do I have in life? Who do I have? What does my future even look like?

Six months earlier, I had lost my sister. Now I was standing on the edge myself. I thought about my girlfriend and my father. My girlfriend did not deserve this kind of pain. My father did not deserve to lose both his children in one year.

When the doctor returned, I told him to do whatever they had to do.

That night at nine o’clock, I was loaded into an ambulance and taken to another hospital. From the ambulance straight into theatre. First, they inserted a shunt to drain fluid from my brain to my stomach, reducing the pressure. Even after that surgery, I already felt some relief and still had a bit of vision left.

A week later, they removed the tumor.

After the second surgery, I spent another three days in the ICU. When I was moved out of ICU, I was still not in the right state of mind at all. The side effects were heavy, confusing, and overwhelming. Days blurred together.

Then one morning, after most of the side effects had started to fade, I woke up and reached for my water bottle. It was supposed to be standing on the bedside table. Before the tumor was removed, I could still see it. Not clearly, but enough. I could see colors, rough shapes, outlines.

This time, there was nothing.

Just darkness.

I could still hear the nurses moving around, so I asked them to switch on the light. They laughed gently and told me it was sleep time, that the lights were already off.

That is when it hit me.

The lights were not off.

My sight was gone.

A week later, they confirmed it. The tumor had been removed, but my vision was gone.

The tumor turned out to be a type of cancer. The doctors rushed through more tests to see if I needed chemo therapy. Thankfully, I did not. They managed to remove everything, and it was a slow-growing type. I could have had it my whole life, even from birth.

After the surgery, I was like a baby. I could not walk. I could not properly understand people. They had to speak very slowly so my brain could keep up. The doctors genuinely thought I might never recover.

Within a month, I could walk again. I could eat by myself. My balance was still off, but I could walk. They trained me how to identify money, how to dress myself, how to make my bed, and how to live as a blind person.

I adapted fairly quickly. Faster than most people.
